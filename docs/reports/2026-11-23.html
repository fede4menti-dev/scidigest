Engineered light-powered enzyme steers a tricky ring-forming reaction

Electrocyclizations are reactions where an open chain of molecules curls up and closes into a ring. They can be triggered by heat or light, and the direction the ends twist as they join decides which mirror-image product you get.

Nature Chemistry · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. PYMOL depiction of SHOC2 protein crystal structure · Achaparro0600, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “An artificial photoenzyme constrains substrates for selective antarafacial electrocyclizations”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Electrocyclizations are reactions where an open chain of molecules curls up and closes into a ring. They can be triggered by heat or light, and the direction the ends twist as they join decides which mirror-image product you get. Chemists rarely have enzymes that can control these light-driven versions, and it hasn't been clear what an enzyme would need to do to catalyse them well.

Starting from a protein that had already been engineered to perform a different light-driven ring-forming reaction ([2+2] cycloaddition), the researchers introduced ten amino-acid changes to make a new enzyme they call PEC1.3. It converts a range of acrylanilide starting materials into ring products with high yields and tight control over both where and in which 3D orientation the ring forms. A very high-resolution (1.3 ångström) crystal structure showed the substrate sitting in just the right spot to receive energy from a built-in benzophenone group that absorbs light and passes the excitation on. Surprisingly, the hydrogen bonds that mattered for the parent enzyme were lost during engineering; instead a mesh of greasy, water-avoiding contacts clamps the substrate into the reactive shape — so firmly that the enzyme still works at extremely cold temperatures where molecules barely move.[1]

Why it matters

Most enzyme design relies on polar contacts like hydrogen bonds to hold substrates in place, which limits chemists to molecules that carry such chemical handles. This work shows that purely hydrophobic packing can enforce precise reaction outcomes, opening a route to selective catalysis on plain, unfunctionalised molecules. It also expands the small set of enzymes that can harness light to build rings.

Caveats

The work was tested on a specific family of substrates (acrylanilides), so how broadly the hydrophobic-clamping strategy transfers to other reactions or molecules remains to be shown.

Key terms
Electrocyclization:
A reaction in which an open chain of carbon atoms closes into a ring by forming a new bond between its two ends.
6π-electrocyclization:
A ring closure involving six electrons in the reacting system.
Antarafacial:
A geometry where the new bond forms across opposite faces of the molecule, which fixes the 3D shape of the product.
Photoenzyme:
An enzyme that uses absorbed light energy to drive its reaction.
Triplet sensitizer:
A group (here benzophenone) that absorbs light and hands the excitation energy to the substrate.
Hydrophobic interactions:
Attractions between water-avoiding, non-polar parts of molecules.

Published today, this engineered artificial photoenzyme catalyses selective antarafacial electrocyclizations, a reaction class that enzymes rarely perform, and shows how the protein pre-organizes its substrate.

Farming intensification in Sumatra changes which species live where, not just how many

Demand for farm products worldwide has grown over recent decades, pushing more intensive land use into some of the planet's most species-rich tropical regions.

Nature · Nov 18, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Sumatra oil palm plantation · Firzafp, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Tropical land-use intensification reshapes biodiversity in expected and unexpected ways”
Published Nov 18, 2026

Demand for farm products worldwide has grown over recent decades, pushing more intensive land use into some of the planet's most species-rich tropical regions. This piece in Nature describes a study that asked how this shift affects living things across the whole range of life, from microbes in the soil up to animals with backbones.

The researchers assessed around 40,000 species on the Indonesian island of Sumatra. They report that more intensive agriculture harms biodiversity in two ways: some species disappear, and the mix of species that make up local communities changes. These effects showed up at different spatial scales, from local sites to wider landscapes, and some of the patterns matched expectations while others did not.[2]

Why it matters

Most studies of land-use change look at a few well-known groups such as birds or mammals. Covering tens of thousands of species, including tiny soil organisms, gives a fuller picture of what tropical farming expansion costs nature. It could guide how farmland is planned in biodiversity hotspots.

Caveats

The summary covers a single region of Indonesia and doesn't say which effects were unexpected. It also doesn't show how well the results apply to other tropical areas.

Land-use intensification:
Turning land into more heavily managed, higher-output uses, for example replacing forest with intensively farmed plantations.
Biodiversity:
The variety of living things in an area, including how many species there are and how different they are from one another.
Community composition:
Which particular species are present together in a place, not just how many there are.
Spatial scale:
The size of the area being studied, from a single plot up to a whole region.

A survey of some 40,000 species in Sumatra, from soil microbes to vertebrates, shows how agricultural intensification erodes tropical biodiversity across spatial scales.

El Niño years made famines more likely and longer in Europe, 1500–1800

El Niño is the warm phase of a Pacific Ocean climate cycle that shifts weather patterns around the world from one year to the next.

Nature Food · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Painting, miniature; Miniatures · Piat Joseph Sauvage, via Wikimedia Commons · CC0

Original title: “El Niño amplified food insecurity in early modern Europe from 1500 to 1800”
Published Nov 23, 2026

El Niño is the warm phase of a Pacific Ocean climate cycle that shifts weather patterns around the world from one year to the next. Researchers asked whether this distant ocean cycle affected food shortages in Europe between roughly 1500 and 1800, a period when harvests were highly vulnerable to bad weather. They compared historical records of 160 European famine episodes with reconstructions of when El Niño events occurred.

They found a consistent link between El Niño years and food crises. Central Europe was the region whose climate was most strongly tied to El Niño, and there El Niño raised the chance that a famine would begin. More than 40% of famine starts in that region coincided with an El Niño event. El Niño also made famines last longer across all nine European regions with enough data: during El Niño years, an ongoing famine was 24% less likely to come to an end.[3]

Why it matters

The study suggests that climate swings originating in the tropical Pacific could reach Europe and affect whether people had enough to eat centuries ago. It adds historical evidence that global climate patterns can influence regional food security. This is relevant to understanding how food systems respond to climate variability today.

Caveats

This is a statistical association drawn from historical records, so it shows correspondence rather than directly proving the weather mechanism. The effect on famine starts was clearest only in central Europe, and the analysis depends on how complete the famine records and El Niño reconstructions are.

Key terms
El Niño/Southern Oscillation (ENSO):
A natural climate cycle in the tropical Pacific that alternates between warmer (El Niño) and cooler (La Niña) ocean conditions every few years, changing weather worldwide.
Teleconnection:
A link between climate in one part of the world and weather in a distant region.
Interannual variability:
Changes in climate from one year to the next, as opposed to long-term trends.
Subsistence crisis:
A period when a population cannot produce or obtain enough basic food, such as a famine.
Famine onset:
The start of a famine episode.
Why it was picked

Published today, an analysis of 160 European famine episodes links El Niño events to the onset and duration of famines between 1500 and 1800.

Faulty RNA editing in blood rises with age and partly runs in families

Before a gene's message (RNA) is used to build a protein, non-coding stretches called introns are normally cut out and the remaining pieces joined in different combinations, a process called alternative splicing.

Nature Communications · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. A scientist checks a blood sample. · National Eye Institute, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 2.0

Original title: “Longitudinal changes in mis-splicing in a cohort of female twins are widespread and highlight mechanisms of ageing”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Before a gene's message (RNA) is used to build a protein, non-coding stretches called introns are normally cut out and the remaining pieces joined in different combinations, a process called alternative splicing. This study asked how splicing changes as people get older. The researchers analysed RNA sequencing data from the whole blood of 318 female twins, whose median age was 61. Each woman was sampled three times over a period of 6 to 8 years, so changes could be followed within the same people over time.

Several types of splicing shifted over the follow-up period. The clearest change was a rise in intron retention, where introns are left in the RNA by mistake, which is a sign of faulty splicing. Comparing identical and non-identical twins showed that a person's level of intron retention is partly inherited, and the inherited share grew from about 0.44 to 0.55 over time. People with more intron retention tended to have lower levels of several RNA-binding proteins (SRSF4, ELAVL1 and AGO1). Intron retention was also linked to diabetes and to markers of kidney disease, and the strength of these links changed across the time points.[4]

Why it matters

Following the same people over years shows that splicing errors build up with age, rather than just differing between younger and older groups. The links to diabetes and kidney markers point to splicing errors as a possible piece of how ageing connects to chronic disease, and the RNA-binding proteins identified suggest where to look for causes.

Caveats

The cohort contained only women and only blood was studied, so it is unclear whether the results hold for men or other tissues. The links with RNA-binding proteins and disease are associations and do not show what causes what.

Key terms
Alternative splicing:
The cutting and rejoining of an RNA message in different ways, so one gene can produce several versions of a protein.
Intron retention:
When a stretch of RNA that should be removed (an intron) is left in, usually a sign of a splicing error.
RNA-seq:
A method that reads all the RNA molecules in a sample to measure which genes are active and how their RNA is spliced.
Heritability:
The share of differences between people in a trait that can be explained by genetic differences, here estimated by comparing identical and non-identical twins.
Longitudinal study:
A study that measures the same people repeatedly over time.
RNA-binding proteins:
Proteins that attach to RNA and help control how it is processed, including splicing.
Why it was picked

Published today, longitudinal data from female twins reveal a widespread rise in splicing errors over six to eight years, pointing to molecular mechanisms of ageing.

People born later reach old age with sharper memories that fade more slowly

Over the past few decades, the chance of developing dementia at a given age has dropped in Europe and the United States.

Nature Medicine · Nov 17, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Elderly memory test · President Of Ukraine from Україна, via Wikimedia Commons · CC0

Original title: “Generational gains in cognitive capacity and dementia incidence rates in Western countries”
Published Nov 17, 2026

Over the past few decades, the chance of developing dementia at a given age has dropped in Europe and the United States. The researchers asked why: do newer generations start old age with better memory, lose memory more slowly, or both? They pooled about 783,000 word-list memory tests from roughly 219,000 adults aged 50 and over in five long-running studies (from England, the US, Ireland, the Netherlands and across Europe), some followed for up to 30 years. For people tested at least four times, they estimated each person's memory level and how fast it was changing, then compared people born between 1910 and 1964.

Later-born generations had both advantages. Each decade of later birth meant about half a word more recalled on a 20-word scale, plus slightly slower yearly decline. Gains were biggest among the fastest decliners, and later-born people spent more time in stable stretches with no memory loss. Projected forward, two generations born 20 years apart would differ by about 5 words at age 80, roughly half from starting level and half from slower decline. That gap is large enough to plausibly explain the reported 13% drop in dementia incidence per decade. Faster memory decline also tracked with higher rates of clinical impairment and, in a separate brain-scan group of about 1,900 people, faster shrinkage of the hippocampus. General calendar-year changes affecting everyone at once did not appear to explain the differences.[5]

Why it matters

Memory level seems to be built mostly in childhood, while the speed of decline is shaped by conditions in adulthood, such as blood pressure control or smoking. So keeping dementia rates falling likely needs brain-health efforts across the whole life, starting early, not just in midlife or old age. The authors warn these gains could stall if childhood cognitive improvements or adult health worsen.

Caveats

This is an observational study, and age, birth year and calendar year are mathematically intertwined, so separating generational effects from calendar-time trends relies on assumptions. Fast decliners may also have been more likely to drop out before four tests, the link to dementia rates is a projection rather than a direct measurement, and this summary is based on a preprint whose numbers may differ in the final published paper.

Key terms
Episodic memory:
Memory for specific events or information, here tested by asking people to recall a list of words.
Birth cohort:
A group of people born in the same span of years, used to compare generations.
Dementia incidence:
How many new cases of dementia appear in a population over a given time.
Hippocampus:
A brain region essential for forming memories; its shrinkage (atrophy) is linked to Alzheimer's disease.
Mild cognitive impairment (MCI):
Noticeable thinking or memory problems that are not yet severe enough to count as dementia.
Period effect:
A change tied to the calendar year that affects all ages at once, as opposed to effects tied to when someone was born.
Why it was picked

Thirty years of European and US cohort data show later-born generations have higher cognitive capacity, which may explain falling dementia incidence.

In richer countries, food's toll on wildlife stops rising with wealth

As countries get richer, people usually eat more and buy different foods, which can mean more farmland and more harm to wild species.

Nature Food · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Cattle herd grazing in pasture in autumn, Fillmore County, Minnesota, USA · Y1997xf11, via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “Biodiversity footprints of food consumption decouple from economic growth at high income levels”
Published Nov 23, 2026

As countries get richer, people usually eat more and buy different foods, which can mean more farmland and more harm to wild species. This study asked whether the damage to biodiversity linked to a country's food consumption keeps growing alongside its economy, or whether the two eventually separate. The researchers examined data from 158 countries covering a 25-year period, tracking each country's 'biodiversity footprint': the estimated harm to species caused by producing the food that country's people eat, wherever in the world that food is grown.

They found that at higher income levels, the biodiversity footprint of food was more likely to 'decouple' from economic growth, meaning the economy could keep expanding without the footprint rising at the same pace. This separation was stronger for plant-based foods than for animal-based foods. Meat from ruminants (grazing animals with multi-chambered stomachs, such as cattle and sheep) showed the weakest decoupling.[6]

Why it matters

Global food production is a major driver of biodiversity loss, so knowing whether wealth growth must bring more damage helps guide policy. The results suggest richer economies can reduce the link, but that animal products, especially beef and lamb, remain a sticking point that may need targeted attention.

Caveats

The summary says decoupling is 'more likely' at high incomes, not guaranteed, and does not say whether footprints actually fall in absolute terms; it is an observational analysis, so it cannot prove that wealth itself causes the change.

Key terms
Biodiversity footprint:
An estimate of the harm to species and ecosystems caused by producing the goods (here, food) that a population consumes, including impacts in other countries through trade.
Decoupling:
When an economy grows while an environmental impact stays flat, grows more slowly, or falls, rather than rising in step.
Ruminant meat:
Meat from cud-chewing grazing animals such as cattle, sheep and goats, which typically need a lot of land.
Plant-based vs animal-based foods:
Foods coming from crops (grains, vegetables, fruit) compared with foods from livestock (meat, dairy, eggs).
Why it was picked

Published today, a 158-country, 25-year analysis shows food-related biodiversity footprints decouple from economic growth at high incomes, more strongly for plant-based foods than for ruminant meat.

Chemists finally bottle oxoboranes, long-sought molecules with a boron–oxygen triple bond

Oxoboranes are molecules in which a boron atom is bonded to only two neighbouring atoms, one of them an oxygen held by a triple bond.

Nature Chemistry · Nov 20, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Boron crystals · SJ879 C, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 4.0

Original title: “Isolable crystalline oxoboranes”
Published Nov 20, 2026

Oxoboranes are molecules in which a boron atom is bonded to only two neighbouring atoms, one of them an oxygen held by a triple bond. They are extremely reactive, so until now they have usually been seen only fleetingly, as short-lived intermediates, rather than as substances that can be collected and studied. Chemists have tried to make them since the 1960s, but isolating them as solids or in solution has been very difficult.

This piece reports that two separate research groups have now succeeded in isolating oxoboranes as stable crystals. One group made a neutral (uncharged) version and the other a positively charged (cationic) version. Both started from related precursor molecules called diboracumulenes, which contain a chain of boron atoms linked by double bonds. The description here is a short summary, so details of how the molecules were stabilised are not given.[7]

Why it matters

Having oxoboranes in a bottle lets chemists directly measure the structure and bonding of a boron–oxygen triple bond, a basic question in how main-group elements bond. Stable versions could also be explored as building blocks or reagents in boron chemistry.

Caveats

The summary is brief and does not explain how stable the compounds are, what conditions they need, or how they react, so their practical usefulness remains unclear.

Key terms
Oxoborane:
A molecule containing a boron atom triple-bonded to an oxygen atom, with boron attached to only one other group.
Dicoordinate boron:
A boron atom directly bonded to just two other atoms.
Condensed phase:
A liquid or solid state, as opposed to isolated molecules in a gas or trapped at very low temperature.
Diboracumulene:
A molecule with two boron atoms in a chain of consecutive double bonds; here it serves as the starting material.
Cationic:
Carrying an overall positive electric charge.
Why it was picked

Two independent studies isolate crystalline oxoboranes with B–O triple bonds, ending a synthetic quest that dates back to the 1960s.

Inherited ALK mutation stalls nerve-cell development, setting stage for neuroblastoma

Nature Communications · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Induced pluripotent stem cells · NIH Image Gallery from Bethesda, Maryland, USA, via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “ALK R1275Q expands Schwann cell precursor-like cells during sympathoadrenal commitment, creating a permissive state for neuroblastoma initiation”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Neuroblastoma is a childhood cancer that starts in cells of the sympathoadrenal lineage — nerve-and-adrenal-forming cells that develop from the embryonic neural crest. Some families carry an inherited mutation in the ALK gene (R1275Q) that raises the risk of this cancer, but it has been unclear what that mutation actually does during early human development. The researchers took skin-type cells from patients with the mutation and reprogrammed them into induced pluripotent stem cells, then guided those stem cells down the sympathoadrenal developmental path in the lab.

Cells carrying ALK R1275Q matured more slowly and stayed stuck in a dividing progenitor state, with higher ALK signalling and an expanded group of cells resembling Schwann cell precursors (an early neural-crest-derived cell type). Blocking ALK with a drug, or using CRISPR gene editing to repair the mutation, shrank this population. On its own the mutation did not produce tumours in animals, but combined with the cancer gene MYCN it made cells more prone to becoming tumours.[8]

Why it matters

It suggests that an inherited cancer-risk mutation acts long before a tumour appears, by freezing developing cells in an immature, dividing state that is easier to transform. That reframes neuroblastoma as partly a disorder of derailed development, and points to ALK-blocking drugs as a way to push those cells back onto a normal path.

Caveats

Most of the work is in lab-grown stem cell models rather than real developing embryos, and the mutation alone did not cause tumours, so other cooperating events are needed. How well these findings translate to patients remains to be shown.

Neural crest:
A migratory group of embryonic cells that gives rise to nerves, pigment cells and parts of the adrenal gland.
Sympathoadrenal lineage:
The developmental branch producing sympathetic nerve cells and adrenal hormone-producing cells; neuroblastoma arises here.
iPSC:
Induced pluripotent stem cell — an adult cell reprogrammed back to a stem-cell state so it can be grown into other cell types in the lab.
Schwann cell precursor (SCP):
An early neural-crest-derived cell type that can become several different mature cell types.
ALK R1275Q:
A specific inherited change in the ALK gene that keeps its growth-signalling activity switched on.
MYCN:
A gene that drives cell proliferation and is frequently amplified in aggressive neuroblastoma.

Self-correcting quantum memory outlasts the best uncorrected qubit in its own device

Quantum bits lose their fragile information quickly, so quantum computers need error correction.

Nature Physics · Nov 16, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. A niobium 1.3 GHz nine-cell superconducting radio frequency microwave cavity at Fermilab . · Z22, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Extending qubit lifetime beyond break-even and enhancing quantum sensing by autonomous quantum error correction”
Published Nov 16, 2026

Quantum bits lose their fragile information quickly, so quantum computers need error correction. Usually this means repeatedly measuring the system for signs of an error and then applying a fix, which requires extra hardware and can itself introduce mistakes. A gentler alternative is 'autonomous' error correction, where the fix happens automatically with no measurement or feedback. Earlier autonomous schemes had not clearly reached 'break-even', meaning the protected qubit lived longer than the best unprotected qubit available in the same device.

The team stored a qubit as specific patterns of microwave photons in a superconducting aluminium cavity. When a photon leaked out, a carefully shaped microwave pulse moved the error onto a helper (ancilla) qubit and restored the stored state. The helper was then reset in about 190 nanoseconds by dumping its energy into a deliberately leaky resonator, with roughly 99.6% success. With repeated correction cycles, the protected qubit lasted about 2.0 ms. That is 18% longer than the best unprotected option (a qubit made from 0 or 1 photon), 3.3 times longer than the same code without correction, and 15 times longer than the helper qubit alone. Using the same method to measure a tiny frequency shift, they gained 6.3 dB in measurement information compared with the simple 0-or-1-photon state.[9]

Why it matters

This shows that the break-even point can be passed without measuring errors or using real-time feedback, which could make error correction simpler and cheaper in hardware. It also suggests that error correction can help quantum sensors stay precise for longer.

Caveats

The 18% gain is modest, and only single-photon loss was targeted in one small device; scaling up to full fault-tolerant computing remains open. The text is a preprint that had not been peer reviewed, so numbers in the final published paper may differ.

Key terms
Logical qubit:
A qubit stored redundantly across a larger system so that errors can be spotted and undone.
Break-even point:
When an error-corrected qubit survives longer than the best uncorrected qubit in the same system.
Autonomous quantum error correction:
Error correction that runs automatically through built-in interactions and engineered energy loss, with no measurements or decisions made by a computer.
Binomial code:
A way of encoding a qubit in particular photon-number combinations (here 0+4 photons and 2 photons), so that losing one photon can be detected and reversed.
Ancilla:
A helper qubit that absorbs the error information and is then reset so it can be used again.
Fisher information:
A measure of how precisely an experiment can estimate a quantity, here a small frequency shift.
Why it was picked

Measurement-free autonomous error correction extends a bosonic logical qubit's lifetime beyond the best physical qubit in the same system, with added prospects for quantum sensing.

Controlling light's phase on chips by tuning losses instead of refractive index

Programmable photonic circuits, chips that route and process light, usually steer light by changing the refractive index of a material (how much it slows light).

Original title: “Non Hermitian synthetic phase shifter with topologically protected phase control via tunable losses”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Programmable photonic circuits, chips that route and process light, usually steer light by changing the refractive index of a material (how much it slows light). Doing this is one of the things that makes it hard to build these circuits much larger. The researchers asked whether a different control knob, the amount of light the circuit deliberately loses, could do the same job.

They built a framework for a 'synthetic' phase shifter that adjusts the phase of light, meaning where it sits in its wave cycle, by modulating optical loss rather than refractive index. According to the publisher's summary, the approach gives control over a full cycle of phase, and that control is protected by topological quantities. The authors report both developing and validating the approach, though the short description does not give details of the experiments or performance figures.[14]

Why it matters

Phase shifters are basic building blocks of programmable photonic chips, which are being explored for communications, computing and sensing. A method that avoids the usual refractive-index route, and whose phase control is robust because it rests on topology, could help these chips scale to larger sizes.

Caveats

The available description is very brief, so the scale of the demonstration, its speed and energy use, and how it compares with conventional phase shifters are not stated.

Key terms
Non-Hermitian:
Describes physical systems that exchange energy with their surroundings, for example by losing or gaining light, rather than conserving it perfectly.
Phase shifter:
A component that shifts how far along its wave cycle a light wave is, used to control how light beams combine and interfere.
Refractive-index modulation:
Changing how strongly a material slows down light, the usual way to tune photonic circuits.
Topologically protected:
A property tied to a whole-number feature of the system, like a winding count, so small imperfections cannot easily change it.
Optical loss:
Light that is absorbed or leaks away instead of travelling on through the circuit.

It resolves a question in combinatorics on words about how much freedom is needed to avoid repetitions, pinning the answer at three. It also gives a practical, efficient algorithm that works even against an adversary choosing the lists on the fly.

Mechanistic interpretability tries to explain what large AI models are doing inside, and that work feeds into safety and trust. The experiments cover only two tasks and linear alignment maps, so it is unclear how often parasite features show up in other settings or in other methods. The work is a non-peer-reviewed preprint.

The statistical link was moderate (standardized effects of about 0.25 and 0.30), with confidence ranges well above zero.[39]

Why it matters

The results suggest that the similarities in vocabulary across the world's languages come partly from people in different cultures talking about similar things with similar frequency. This links everyday language use to the long-term shape of what languages choose to name.

Caveats

This is a correlational analysis, so it shows that frequency predicts whether a word exists but does not prove that frequency causes it. Frequency explained only part of the pattern, and the frequency data came from a sample of 46 languages.

Complete pig genomes reveal moving centromeres and genes behind high-altitude adaptation

Most pig genetics relies on a single reference genome that still has gaps, especially in hard-to-read repetitive regions such as centromeres (the chromosome 'waist' that guides chromosome separation during cell division).

Nature Genetics · Nov 16, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Tibetan pig · Popolon, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

Original title: “High-quality pig pan-genome resources illuminate centromere evolution and enhance genotype–phenotype mapping”
Published Nov 16, 2026

Most pig genetics relies on a single reference genome that still has gaps, especially in hard-to-read repetitive regions such as centromeres (the chromosome 'waist' that guides chromosome separation during cell division). The researchers assembled genomes from 19 pig breeds chosen from different climates, including cold-tolerant, heat- and humidity-tolerant and Tibetan high-altitude pigs. Three of these are telomere-to-telomere (T2T), meaning every chromosome is read end to end with no gaps. They paired the genomes with RNA and metabolite measurements from nine tissues, protein-binding maps that locate centromeres, and 3D chromosome-contact data.

In 12 boars, the Y-chromosome centromere sat in an unusual non-repetitive stretch of DNA. Its exact position split the breeds into two groups that matched their family tree and roughly fell south and north of the Yangtze River. Centromere position tracked how far a nearby repeated DNA block had expanded. Chromosome 1 centromeres shifted often, and a highly active gene, eEF1A1, sits near chromosome breakage and fusion points in several mammals. Combining 31 pig genomes gave about 230,000 large DNA changes (structural variants). One of them, a 300-base insertion in front of the AKR1B1 gene in Tibetan pigs, reduced gene activity in a reporter test. Lowering AKR1B1 in pig kidney cells grown at low oxygen increased cell growth and reduced cell death. A graph-style pan-genome built on a T2T reference explained slightly more of the inherited variation in about 2,000 molecular traits measured in 334 Yorkshire pigs.[40]

Why it matters

These gap-free genomes give pig breeders and biomedical researchers a more accurate reference, including regions that were previously unreadable. The Y-chromosome centromere is passed only from father to son, so its position could serve as a stable marker of pig ancestry. Including large DNA changes, not just single-letter differences, may help link genes to traits for breeding.

Caveats

The AKR1B1 role in altitude adaptation rests on cell-culture and reporter experiments, not tests in living pigs. The authors note that sorting out how environment, domestication and Y-chromosome decay each drive centromere movement will require more breeds. The eEF1A1 link to chromosome breakage is a correlation-based hypothesis.

Key terms
Pan-genome:
A combined genome collection from many individuals or breeds, capturing variation that a single reference genome misses.
Telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome:
A genome assembly in which each chromosome is fully read from one end to the other with no gaps.
Centromere repositioning:
A shift in where the centromere sits along a chromosome during evolution.
Structural variant:
A large DNA change, such as an insertion, deletion or flipped segment, typically 50 bases or longer.
Heritability:
The share of variation in a trait across individuals that can be explained by genetic differences.
Hypoxia:
A low-oxygen condition, such as at high altitude.
