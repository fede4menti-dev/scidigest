Paper wasps have brain cells tuned to their nestmates' faces

Some paper wasps (the species Polistes fuscatus) can tell each other apart by the distinctive markings on their faces, much as people recognise one another by facial features.

Cell · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. French paper wasp ( Polistes gallicus ) female, Malta. · Charles J. Sharp, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Neural correlates of facial recognition in a social wasp”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Some paper wasps (the species Polistes fuscatus) can tell each other apart by the distinctive markings on their faces, much as people recognise one another by facial features. Humans and other primates have so-called face cells: neurons in visual areas of the brain that respond strongly to faces. The researchers asked whether these wasps, despite having a tiny and very differently built brain, have something similar.

According to the publisher's summary, they found neurons they call "wasp cells" in a brain region known as the lateral protocerebrum. These cells respond in ways that distinguish one facial pattern from another, which matches what the wasps need to do when they identify social partners. The short description does not explain how the recordings were made or how many cells or animals were studied.[1]

Why it matters

The finding suggests that insects and primates, which are only very distantly related, may have independently arrived at a similar brain solution for recognising individuals by face. It offers a much smaller nervous system in which to study how face recognition works.

Caveats

The available summary is very brief and gives no details on methods, sample size, or how specifically these cells respond to faces compared with other images, so the strength of the comparison to primate face cells is hard to judge from it alone.

Key terms
Face cells:
Neurons in primate visual brain areas that fire strongly when the animal sees a face.
Polistes fuscatus:
A species of paper wasp whose members recognise each other individually by their unique face markings.
Lateral protocerebrum:
A region of the insect brain that receives and processes information, including visual signals.
Wasp cells:
The authors' name for neurons in the wasp brain that respond differently to different wasp facial patterns.
Why it was picked

A first discovery of face-selective 'wasp cells' in an insect brain shows that face-recognition circuitry evolved convergently far outside primates.

Brains of speakers of 21 languages appear to share a common meaning signal

Do people who speak very different languages represent the meaning of what they hear or read in a similar way in the brain?

Nature Neuroscience · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Fmri brain scanner · MRI_brain.jpg : created by NASA + Rami Tarawneh, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “A shared meaning component across languages in the human brain”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Do people who speak very different languages represent the meaning of what they hear or read in a similar way in the brain? To test this, de Varda and colleagues used fMRI (brain scans that track changes in blood flow as a stand-in for neural activity) collected from speakers of 21 languages. They combined these scans with multilingual language models, which are AI systems trained on text from many languages, and built encoding models: statistical tools that learn to predict a person's brain activity from features the AI extracts from the language input.

The central test was whether an encoding model trained only on some languages could predict brain responses to languages it had never been trained on. According to the publisher's summary, it could. The authors take this as evidence that part of the brain's response to language reflects meaning shared across languages, not just features specific to each language.[2]

Why it matters

The result suggests that, beneath the large differences in sounds, words and grammar, human brains may handle meaning in a partly common way. It also shows that multilingual AI models can serve as a tool for comparing how the brain processes many languages at once.

Caveats

Only a brief summary is available, so the size of the effect, the number of participants and how much of the brain response is shared versus language-specific cannot be judged here. The findings depend on how well the AI model's representations capture meaning.

Key terms
fMRI:
Functional magnetic resonance imaging, a brain-scanning method that measures blood-flow changes as an indirect sign of which brain areas are active.
Multilingual language model:
An AI system trained on text in many languages that turns words and sentences into numerical representations.
Encoding model:
A model that learns to predict measured brain activity from features of a stimulus, here taken from a language model.
Generalization to unseen languages:
Testing whether a model trained on some languages still makes accurate predictions for languages left out of its training.

fMRI from speakers of 21 languages shows brain encoding models transfer to unseen languages, evidence for a shared meaning code across human languages.

Bumpy 'egg-box' surface helps solid-state batteries discharge faster without failing

Solid-state batteries replace the liquid inside a normal battery with a solid ceramic, and pairing that solid with a lithium metal electrode could store much more energy.

Nature Energy · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. All-solid-state battery showing the solid-state electrolyte · Luca Bertoli, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Contouring mitigates voiding at the lithium/ceramic electrolyte interface on discharging solid-state batteries”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Solid-state batteries replace the liquid inside a normal battery with a solid ceramic, and pairing that solid with a lithium metal electrode could store much more energy. A known problem is that when these batteries discharge, lithium is pulled away from the boundary between the metal and the ceramic faster than it can be replaced, leaving gaps called voids. These voids reduce contact, raise resistance and can cause the cell to fail. The researchers asked whether reshaping that boundary could help, so they gave it an 'egg-box' pattern of bumps and dips instead of a flat surface, using a sulfur-based ceramic electrolyte (Li6PS5Cl).

With the contoured surface, voids only began forming at a discharge current about ten times higher than for a flat surface (2 versus 0.2 mA per square centimetre, at 2 MPa pressure and 30 °C). The patterned cells also handled short bursts of current up to 3 mA cm−2 at 30 °C and 8 mA cm−2 at 60 °C without failing. Voids still appeared, but they grew less, and the voltage losses linked to them were smaller. The contoured cells kept running under discharge patterns modelled on a standard car driving test, while flat-interface cells failed quickly. A full battery cell with a thin contoured electrolyte showed the same benefit.[3]

Why it matters

Electric vehicles demand bursts of high current, and void formation has been one barrier stopping lithium metal solid-state batteries from meeting that demand. Changing only the shape of the interface, rather than the materials, offers a relatively simple route toward practical discharge rates.

Caveats

The contouring reduces but does not fully stop void formation, and the results come from lab cells tested at specific pressures and temperatures. How well this holds up over long-term cycling or in commercial-scale cells is not addressed in the abstract.

Key terms
Solid-state battery:
A battery that uses a solid material instead of a liquid to carry ions between its electrodes.
Solid electrolyte (Li6PS5Cl):
A ceramic containing lithium, phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine that lets lithium ions move through it.
Void formation:
Gaps that open between the lithium metal and the electrolyte when lithium is removed during discharge, cutting the contact area.
Voltage polarization:
An extra voltage loss caused by resistance in the cell, here made worse by voids.
Egg-box topography:
A surface patterned with a regular grid of bumps and hollows, like an egg carton, which increases contact area.
WLTP:
Worldwide Harmonized Light Vehicles Test Procedure, a standard driving cycle used to test vehicles; here it was turned into realistic current demands.
Why it was picked

A contoured lithium/electrolyte interface raises the voiding threshold tenfold and lets solid-state batteries handle realistic EV discharge, removing a key practical barrier.

Zhang, S., Hu, B., Melvin, D. L. R., Spencer-Jolly, D., Gao, X., Wang, L., Yang, L., Monroe, C. W., & Bruce, P. G. (2026). Contouring mitigates voiding at the lithium/ceramic electrolyte interface on discharging solid-state batteries. Nature Energy. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41560-026-02172-w

Researchers build a 'pangenome' map covering many mouse strains, not just one

Lab mouse genetics has long relied on a single reference genome taken from one standard strain, called C57BL/6J. Comparing DNA from other mouse strains against this one sequence can miss or distort their differences.

Nature Genetics · Nov 23, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Laboratory mouse · Rama, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 2.0 fr

Original title: “A draft mouse pangenome graph reference”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Lab mouse genetics has long relied on a single reference genome taken from one standard strain, called C57BL/6J. Comparing DNA from other mouse strains against this one sequence can miss or distort their differences. This study asked whether a reference that captures the genetic variety of many mice could do a better job.

The researchers built three pangenomes, which are references that combine many genomes into one connected structure called a graph and are detailed down to individual DNA letters. All three are built on a complete, gap-free 'telomere-to-telomere' assembly of the C57BL/6J genome, used as the backbone. According to the publisher's summary, these resources improve how sequencing reads are lined up against the reference, reduce bias toward the standard strain, make detection of genetic variants more accurate, and support later studies of how genes work. The description gives no specific numbers.[4]

Why it matters

Mice are among the most widely used animals in biomedical research, and many studies compare genetically different strains. A reference that reflects that diversity could make those genetic comparisons more reliable and cut down on errors caused by measuring everything against one strain.

Caveats

The authors describe this as a 'draft' resource. The publisher's summary does not say how many strains are included or how large the improvements are.

Key terms
Pangenome:
A reference that represents the DNA of many individuals or strains together, instead of a single genome.
Graph-based reference:
A way to store genomes as a network: shared DNA forms common paths, and differences between strains appear as alternative branches.
Telomere-to-telomere (T2T):
A genome assembly that is complete from one end of each chromosome to the other, with no gaps.
C57BL/6J:
The most commonly used inbred laboratory mouse strain, whose genome is the standard mouse reference.
Reference bias:
The tendency for analyses to work better for DNA that resembles the reference genome, so differences from it can be missed or misread.
Variant calling:
Identifying where an individual's DNA differs from the reference.
Why it was picked

A telomere-to-telomere-anchored mouse pangenome graph cuts reference bias in the most widely used mammalian model organism.

Original title: “Towards population-specific brain charts across the lifespan”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Doctors use growth charts to judge whether a child's height or weight is typical for their age. Researchers have been building similar "brain charts" that describe how brain structure normally changes from birth to old age, but most of the brain scans behind them come from mainly white populations. This study asked whether a chart built from a different population would look different and work better for that population.

The team used what they describe as the largest available collection of brain structure scans from Chinese people across the whole lifespan: 43,037 participants aged 0 to 100. From these scans they built population-specific brain charts. The typical paths of brain development and ageing in this group differed from those seen in mostly white populations. The tailored charts were better at predicting ordinary traits (phenotypes) and better at picking up the departures from typical brain structure that are linked to disease.[5]

Why it matters

If brain charts are based mainly on one group, they may misjudge what counts as normal for people from other backgrounds. That can make brain-based diagnosis less accurate for them. Population-specific charts could make brain imaging tools fairer and more precise for more people.

Caveats

The summary gives no detail on how large the differences between populations were, or what caused them. Genetics, environment, or differences in scanners and study sites could all play a part. It also does not say which diseases were tested.

Structural neuroimaging:
Brain scans, such as MRI, that capture the brain's anatomy rather than its activity.
Developmental trajectory:
The path a brain measure follows as it changes over a person's life.
Phenotype:
An observable trait of a person, such as a physical or behavioural characteristic.

Adding mRNA 'helper' proteins boosts DNA delivery into cells

Getting therapeutic DNA to work inside cells is hard: the DNA has to cross from the cell's cytoplasm into the nucleus, where genes are read, and cells also have defences that chew up or silence foreign DNA.

Nature Biotechnology · Nov 24, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Lipid nanoparticle · Yurko de la traducción Pitana, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Modular mRNA-encoded helper proteins enhance DNA delivery”
Published Nov 24, 2026

Getting therapeutic DNA to work inside cells is hard: the DNA has to cross from the cell's cytoplasm into the nucleus, where genes are read, and cells also have defences that chew up or silence foreign DNA. This paper describes a system the authors call MEND (mRNA-enhanced DNA delivery), in which a dose of messenger RNA is sent in alongside the DNA. The cell translates that mRNA into short-lived helper proteins that prepare it to handle the incoming DNA better.

The authors describe three kinds of helper protein: ones that help shuttle DNA into the nucleus, ones that adjust the cell's internal machinery or state, and ones that quiet the cell's immune reaction to foreign DNA. Because the helpers are just encoded as mRNA, the approach is modular — different helpers can be mixed and matched. They report increased expression of the delivered gene across several different delivery vehicles and DNA formats, both in cultured cells and in animals using lipid nanoparticles as the carrier.[6]

Why it matters

Many gene therapies and DNA vaccines fail not because the DNA is wrong but because too little of it ever reaches the nucleus and gets read. A plug-in way to temporarily make cells more receptive could lower the dose needed and widen what DNA-based medicines can do. Because the helpers are encoded in mRNA, their effect is transient rather than permanently engineered into the cell.

Caveats

The abstract gives no numbers on how large the improvement is, which animals or tissues were tested, or whether the helper proteins cause side effects; safety and durability in humans remain untested.

Key terms
mRNA:
Messenger RNA: a temporary instruction molecule that cells read to build a protein.
Nuclear translocation:
The movement of molecules such as DNA from the cytoplasm into the cell nucleus, where genes are transcribed.
Transgene expression:
How much protein a cell makes from a gene that was delivered from outside.
Lipid nanoparticle (LNP):
A tiny fatty bubble used to carry nucleic acids into cells; the same type of carrier used in mRNA vaccines.
Modular system:
A design made of interchangeable parts that can be swapped or combined for different purposes.
Why it was picked

Co-delivered mRNA helper proteins boost non-viral DNA delivery across vehicles in vitro and in vivo, a modular advance for gene therapy.

Rearranging the same DNA switch parts decides where genes turn on

Enhancers are short stretches of DNA that act like switches, telling a gene when and in which tissues to turn on. They work by providing landing spots (binding sites) for proteins called transcription factors.

Nature Genetics · Nov 24, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Ciona robusta by Kohji Hotta · Delphine Dauga, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Exhaustive rearrangement of an enhancer reveals that dependency-syntax encodes tissue specificity”
Published Nov 24, 2026

Enhancers are short stretches of DNA that act like switches, telling a gene when and in which tissues to turn on. They work by providing landing spots (binding sites) for proteins called transcription factors. Scientists have long asked whether only the number and type of these landing spots matter, or whether their arrangement (the order, spacing and direction, called 'syntax') also counts. To test this, the researchers built 460,800 versions of one enhancer in embryos of Ciona, a small sea animal. Every version had exactly the same set of binding sites for two transcription factor families, ETS and GATA, with the same binding strengths; only their organization differed.

Despite having identical ingredients, only about 18% of the versions switched a gene on. Simply flipping the direction of one binding site turned 54,000 inactive versions into working enhancers, showing that arrangement follows strict rules. The team found two kinds of arrangements: 'dependent' ones, whose activity relies on where stronger and weaker binding sites are placed, and 'robust' ones, which work no matter where those sites go. Using a new method that reads out enhancer activity in single cells across whole embryos (SEMPRA), they showed that dependent arrangements stay sensitive to each cell's mix of proteins and stay restricted to particular tissues, while robust arrangements switch on broadly.[7]

Why it matters

The results suggest that the layout of binding sites within an enhancer, not just which sites are present, helps decide which tissues a gene is active in. This could help researchers interpret DNA changes in enhancers that alter gene activity and design synthetic enhancers that work only in chosen tissues.

Caveats

The study rearranged a single enhancer built from just two types of binding sites in one organism, so it is unclear how broadly these syntax rules apply to other enhancers or to species such as humans.

Key terms
Enhancer:
A short DNA region that boosts a gene's activity in specific cells or at specific times.
Transcription factor binding site (TFBS):
A short DNA sequence that a gene-regulating protein (transcription factor) attaches to.
Syntax:
The arrangement of binding sites within an enhancer: their order, spacing and orientation.
Affinity:
How strongly a transcription factor sticks to a given binding site.
Massively parallel reporter assay (MPRA):
A method that tests thousands of DNA variants at once by linking each to a measurable signal.
Trans environment:
The set of proteins, such as transcription factors, present in a given cell that act on the DNA.
Ciona:
A sea squirt, a simple marine animal whose embryos are widely used to study gene regulation.
Why it was picked

Exhaustively rearranging an enhancer in whole embryos shows that binding-site order, orientation and spacing encode tissue specificity, settling a long-debated question.

Solvason, J. J., Tellez, K., Lim, F., Wang, Q., Song, B. P., Grudzien, J. L., Le, S. H., Olson, K. M., Jindal, G. A., & Farley, E. K. (2026). Exhaustive rearrangement of an enhancer reveals that dependency-syntax encodes tissue specificity. Nature Genetics. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-026-02838-x

Comparing how well different ocean chemistry tricks pull CO2 from the air

Some proposed climate fixes aim to make the ocean absorb more carbon dioxide by changing its chemistry. One idea, ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE), adds alkaline substances to seawater.

Nature Communications · Nov 24, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Mavericks Surf Contest 2010. · Shalom Jacobovitz, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 3.0

Original title: “Global intercomparison of ocean-based geochemical carbon dioxide removal efficiencies”
Published Nov 24, 2026

Some proposed climate fixes aim to make the ocean absorb more carbon dioxide by changing its chemistry. One idea, ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE), adds alkaline substances to seawater. Another, direct ocean removal (DOR), takes dissolved carbon out of seawater so the ocean then draws more CO2 down from the air. Whether either could work at the scale of billions of tonnes depends on how efficient it is. The researchers built a single framework for judging the efficiency of these approaches. They also covered a third category, called ADA, in which alkalinity and dissolved carbon are added together.

They used a computer model of global ocean chemistry and biology to map how DOR efficiency varies by location and season, and compared those maps with OAE. They then showed that results from separate OAE and DOR simulations can be combined to predict what happens when alkalinity and carbon are changed at the same time. This matters for methods such as spreading crushed rock on land (enhanced rock weathering), adding alkalinity to rivers, and speeding up limestone weathering. In these methods the alkalinity has already absorbed some CO2 before it reaches the sea, so the ocean effectively receives both alkalinity and carbon.[8]

Why it matters

Efficiency determines how much CO2 a removal method really takes out of the air for the effort and cost involved. A common yardstick across ocean and land-based weathering approaches helps researchers and policymakers compare options and decide where and when to deploy them.

Caveats

The findings come from a computer model rather than field measurements, so real-world efficiencies may differ. The abstract does not give specific efficiency numbers or say how uncertain the model results are.

Key terms
Ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE):
Adding alkaline (acid-neutralizing) material to seawater so it can hold more dissolved CO2, which leads the ocean to take more from the air.
Direct ocean removal (DOR):
Extracting dissolved inorganic carbon from seawater. The depleted water then absorbs CO2 from the atmosphere to make up the difference.
Dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC):
The total carbon in seawater held as dissolved CO2, bicarbonate and carbonate.
Combined alkalinity and DIC addition (ADA):
A change in which both alkalinity and dissolved carbon enter the ocean together, as happens with weathered rock carried by rivers.
Enhanced rock weathering:
Spreading finely ground rock, often on land, so it reacts with CO2 faster than it would naturally.
Ocean biogeochemical model:
A computer simulation of how ocean currents, chemistry and life move and transform elements such as carbon.
Why it was picked

A unified global framework compares ocean CO2-removal efficiencies, which is timely for judging alkalinity-enhancement climate strategies.

Zhou, M., Timmermans, M., Yankovsky, E., Tyka, M. D., & Long, M. C. (2026). Global intercomparison of ocean-based geochemical carbon dioxide removal efficiencies. Nature Communications, 17(1), 11411. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-78856-4

Reweighting error-check data makes quantum error correction tolerate more noise

Quantum computers protect information by spreading one 'logical' qubit across many physical qubits and repeatedly checking for errors. The pattern of check results is called the syndrome.

Physical Review Letters · Nov 23, 2026
Read more

Original title: “Syndrome Resampling Enhances Quantum Error Correction Thresholds”
Published Nov 23, 2026

Quantum computers protect information by spreading one 'logical' qubit across many physical qubits and repeatedly checking for errors. The pattern of check results is called the syndrome. Error correction only works if the hardware's error rate is below a 'threshold', which current devices struggle to reach. The authors asked whether existing data could be reused more cleverly to lower logical errors without new hardware or a new decoder (the algorithm that guesses which correction to apply). Their idea, syndrome resampling, starts from the observation that rare syndromes tend to come from large errors that the decoder gets wrong. So they reweight the collected runs, raising each syndrome's probability to a power α, which favours the common syndromes and suppresses the rare ones. They link this to a quantity called the Rényi coherent information, whose phase transitions predict a new, higher threshold for each α.

In computer simulations of surface codes with bit-flip noise, the threshold rose from about 10.75% (no resampling) to about 17.7% for α=2 and about 20.9% for α=3 using the best possible decoder, closely matching theoretical predictions. A faster standard decoder also gained. Combined with another filtering trick based on decoder confidence, logical error rates fell by up to four orders of magnitude. They also showed the method can be estimated from a finite set of runs. Applied to real data from a superconducting-qubit experiment (about 44,000 runs), resampling with α=2 cut the logical error rate by roughly a hundredfold while keeping 40% of runs, compared with only 5% kept when discarding every run that showed any error.[9]

Why it matters

The method works with any decoder and any stabilizer code, needing only statistics of the measured syndromes, so it can be applied to data that experiments already collect. It offers a way to get better logical performance from today's noisy hardware while throwing away far fewer runs than strict filtering.

Caveats

This is a preprint, so numbers in the final published paper may differ. The approach discards some runs, and estimating syndrome probabilities can need exponentially many samples at high noise or large code sizes; most tests used simple bit-flip noise and small codes.

Key terms
Syndrome:
The set of check measurements that reveal whether, and roughly where, errors occurred, without disturbing the stored quantum information.
Threshold:
The physical error rate below which making the code bigger reduces logical errors; above it, bigger codes do worse.
Decoder:
An algorithm that reads the syndrome and decides which correction to apply; maximum likelihood decoding is the optimal but usually expensive version.
Post-selection:
Keeping only runs where no errors were detected and discarding the rest; very accurate but wastes most data as noise grows.
Rényi coherent information:
An information-theory measure of how much quantum information survives noise; its sudden changes mark thresholds.
Surface code:
A widely used error-correcting code that arranges qubits on a 2D grid with local checks.
Why it was picked

A general syndrome-resampling method raises error-correction thresholds, easing the hardware demands for fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Colmenarez, L., Márton, Á., & Müller, M. (2026). Syndrome Resampling Enhances Quantum Error Correction Thresholds. Physical Review Letters, 137(22), 220601. https://doi.org/10.1103/v3kk-rwk8

Light can carry orbital angular momentum, a kind of twist in which the wavefronts spiral around the beam's axis like a corkscrew. A beam shaped this way is called a vortex beam. The researchers asked whether this twist could be transferred into a superconductor, a material that carries electric current with zero resistance. Specifically, they targeted the Higgs mode, a collective oscillation in the strength of the superconducting state, so named because it is related to the Higgs boson of particle physics.

The team shone a terahertz vortex beam onto a superconductor made of niobium nitride (NbN). Terahertz light has frequencies between microwaves and infrared. According to the publisher's short description, the beam stamped its orbital angular momentum onto the Higgs mode. In other words, the superconductor's collective oscillation picked up a large-scale twisted pattern inherited from the light.[10]

Why it matters

The result points to a way of using shaped electromagnetic fields to control quantum states inside materials. Being able to hand light's twist to an excitation of a superconductor could give researchers a new tool for probing and steering these delicate states.

Caveats

Only a one-sentence description is available. Details such as the experimental conditions, how the transfer was detected, and how strong the effect is cannot be assessed from it.

Terahertz:

Original title: “The Rotation Strategy Wins Penney's game for Permutations”
Published Nov 24, 2026

Penney's game for permutations works like this: two players each pick a different ordering pattern of length k (for example, 'small, large, medium' when k=3). Random numbers are then drawn one after another, and whoever's pattern first matches the relative ranking of the latest k numbers wins. Earlier researchers, Elizalde and Lin, guessed that the second player can always beat the first by choosing the first player's pattern 'rotated', meaning the last entry is moved to the front. This preprint, which has not yet been peer reviewed, claims to prove that guess for every pattern length of 3 or more.

The author expresses the second player's advantage as a sum over 'clusters', which are chains of overlapping occurrences of the two patterns, each counted with a plus or minus sign. The key step shows that for any given ordering of the numbers, these signed counts add up to only -1, 0 or 1. That keeps the contribution of very long clusters under control. It gives a general proof for k of 8 or more, showing the rotation wins with probability at least 1/2 + 0.0053/(k+1). Exact computer calculations with fractions cover the cases k = 3 to 7.[11]

Why it matters

The result settles an open conjecture about a classic kind of game: being able to see your opponent's choice can guarantee an advantage. The cluster-counting technique may also help with other problems about when patterns first appear in random sequences.

Caveats

This is a preprint that has not been peer reviewed. The guaranteed edge shown for long patterns is small and shrinks as k grows, and the small cases depend on exact computer calculations.

Continuous distribution:
A way of drawing random numbers where ties essentially never happen, so every run of numbers has a clear ranking.

Moving the last entry of a pattern to the front, turning a1a2...ak into ak a1...a(k-1).
Cluster:

The authors noticed that, for any single step, only a small part of the cache is needed to closely reproduce the model's full attention calculation, but different steps need different parts, so together they end up touching almost the whole cache. That suggests the model should choose what to read fresh at each step rather than throwing parts of the cache away permanently.

Their method, RefineKV, groups cache entries into clusters. Each cluster can either be stood in for by a compact summary or opened up to its original entries. To decide which, the authors worked out a mathematical upper limit on how much error a summary adds to the attention output. That limit depends on two things: how much a cluster matters to the current step (importance) and how well its summary captures its contents (distortion). Tested on two models and three benchmarks, RefineKV kept quality close to full attention while making the attention step up to 7.6 times faster and overall text generation up to 1.66 times faster. This is a preprint that has not yet been peer reviewed.[19]

Why it matters

Handling long inputs such as whole books, codebases or long chats is costly for language models, largely because of repeated reads of the KV cache. A per-step way to compress that cache, grounded in an error bound, could make long-context AI cheaper and quicker without a noticeable drop in output quality.

Caveats

The results come from only two models and three benchmarks in a preprint that has not been peer reviewed. Quality is described as near full attention rather than identical, and the reported speedups may vary with hardware and settings.

Working in mice, they found that a flippase called ATP8B1 and its helper protein TMEM30B are made specifically in outer hair cells, concentrate in the stereocilia, and increase once the cells start detecting sound. When either protein was missing, mice needed louder sounds to produce a brain response, a lipid called phosphatidylserine appeared on the outer surface of the cells, and the outer hair cells quickly died.[24]

Why it matters

The study suggests that keeping the two sides of the hair-cell membrane chemically different is necessary for outer hair cells to survive and for normal hearing. It also identifies TMEM30B as a new gene whose loss causes deafness, which could help explain some inherited hearing loss.

Caveats

The work was done in mice, so how it applies to human hearing remains to be confirmed. The abstract does not show directly that the flippase counteracts TMC1/2 scrambling during sound detection, and that link appears to be the authors' proposed explanation.

Original title: “Developmental bias explains the evolutionary trend towards simple leaf shapes”
Published Nov 24, 2026

Flowering plants have leaves ranging from simple, single blades to complex, divided forms such as compound or deeply lobed leaves. Biologists have long debated how much evolution is steered by natural selection versus developmental bias, meaning the tendency of an organism's growth processes to produce some forms more readily than others. The researchers combined large evolutionary family trees of flowering plants with leaf-shape records from herbarium collections (archives of preserved plant specimens). They confirmed that, over the history of these plants, switches from complex to simple leaves happened much more often than switches the other way.

To test whether this one-way trend could come from development itself, the team took an established computer model of leaf growth and added random mutations to it. The simulation included no natural selection. Even so, the rates of shape change it produced closely matched the rates seen in the real evolutionary data. The authors conclude that the gene networks controlling leaf growth produce simple leaves far more easily than complex ones, and that this built-in bias has strongly shaped how leaf form evolved.[15]

Why it matters

The study suggests that evolutionary trends can arise partly from what development makes easy to produce, not only from what helps survival. The finding parallels earlier work showing similar biases toward simple forms in RNA folding and in how proteins assemble. This hints that a preference for simplicity may be common across many biological systems.

Caveats

The developmental evidence comes from a computer model with selection left out, so the match with real data supports the bias explanation but does not rule out a role for natural selection. The results also depend on how well that one model captures real leaf development.

Published Nov 23, 2026

Fungal infections that shrug off several drugs at once endanger both human health and crops, so researchers need new antifungal compounds and a clear picture of how fungi become resistant. This study used SATAY, a method in baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) where small mobile DNA pieces called transposons are inserted at random across the genome. Sequencing then shows which disrupted or altered genes help cells survive a drug. The team ran these screens against 20 antifungal compounds, picking up mutations that switch genes off (loss-of-function) as well as ones that boost a gene's activity (gain-of-function). For compounds that barely affect ordinary lab yeast, they ran the screens in specially drug-sensitive strains.

The screens turned up many resistance routes that had not been described before and pointed to several different ways the drugs act on cells. Two results stood out. Chitosan, an antifungal found in nature, appears to stick to the cell wall through electrical attraction to sugar-phosphate groups called mannosylphosphate. Losing or changing this transporter gives yeast an easy way to become resistant with little cost to its growth.[26]

Why it matters

The collection of drug-response profiles gives other scientists a resource for studying how cells cope with chemical stress and for working out how new antifungals act. Caveats

The screens were done in baker's yeast, including drug-sensitive lab strains, so it is not yet known how fully these resistance mechanisms carry over to disease-causing fungi in patients or crops.

Lysine, an amino acid from food, is broken down in a chain of chemical steps. In two inherited disorders — ALDH7A1 deficiency (which causes seizures that respond to vitamin B6) and glutaric aciduria type I — an enzyme partway down that chain is missing, so harmful intermediate molecules build up and can damage the nervous system. The researchers asked whether blocking the very first step of lysine breakdown, run by the enzyme AASS, would stop these toxic products forming in the first place.

They used two kinds of gene-silencing molecules — antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNAs — that shut down production of AASS. In cells taken from patients with either disease, the treatment lowered the disease-linked metabolites. Why it matters

This is an example of "substrate reduction" — instead of fixing a broken enzyme, you turn down the supply of the raw material that becomes toxic. The same gene-silencing chemistry is already used in approved liver-targeted drugs, so the approach has a plausible route to the clinic for these rare, currently hard-to-treat conditions.

Caveats

The work is in patient-derived cells and mice only; it shows lower metabolite levels, not proven prevention of neurological damage or safety in people.
