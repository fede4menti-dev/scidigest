Two new four-drug tuberculosis combinations pass an early screening trial

Standard treatment for tuberculosis (TB) uses four drugs (isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, called HRZE) for 24 weeks.

The New England Journal of Medicine · Nov 19, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Infectious Diseases Hospital, Rajshahi was established in 1962. · Nahid Hossain, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Assessing Multiple Treatment Regimens for Tuberculosis”
Published Nov 19, 2026

Standard treatment for tuberculosis (TB) uses four drugs (isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, called HRZE) for 24 weeks. Researchers ran a phase 2 trial, a mid-stage test of how well treatments work and how safe they are, to check whether two experimental drugs, ganfeborole and BTZ-043, could be part of shorter, better regimens. They randomly assigned 401 people with lung TB that still responds to rifampin to one of several groups. One group got standard HRZE for 24 weeks. Another got bedaquiline-delamanid-moxifloxacin for 16 weeks. The rest got one of 10 new four-drug combinations for 16 weeks. The main measure was how fast the time needed for TB bacteria to grow from a patient's sputum (coughed-up mucus) sample got longer over the first 12 weeks. A faster increase suggests fewer live bacteria, so the treatment is clearing infection more quickly.

Two regimens stood out. The first was ganfeborole with bedaquiline, pretomanid and moxifloxacin (BPMG). The second was BTZ-043 with bedaquiline, delamanid and moxifloxacin (BDMT). Compared with HRZE, BDMT's rate was about 20% faster, with a 99% estimated probability of being better. BPMG's rate was about 9% faster, with an 83% probability. Serious side effects (grade 3 or higher) happened in 38% of the BPMG group, 28% of the BDMT group and 24% of the HRZE group. One BDMT patient stopped because of a severe drug reaction. One BTZ-043 combination, which included pyrazinamide, was halted early because it damaged the liver.[1]

Why it matters

TB treatment is long, and shorter, more effective regimens could make it easier for patients to finish therapy. This trial narrows a large set of candidate drug combinations down to two that are worth testing in bigger studies.

Caveats

This was a selection trial built to pick regimens for further study, not to show that they cure TB. It measured bacterial response only over 12 weeks, and for BPMG the credible interval includes no benefit at all. BPMG also had more severe side effects than standard treatment.

Key terms
Time to positivity:
How many days it takes for TB bacteria from a sputum sample to show growth in a lab culture. Longer times suggest fewer live bacteria.
Bayesian posterior probability of superiority:
A statistical estimate of how likely it is that a new regimen truly works better than the standard one, given the data collected.
Credible interval:
A range of values within which the true result very likely lies, according to a Bayesian analysis.
Grade 3 adverse event:
A severe side effect, though not immediately life-threatening.
Hepatotoxic:
Harmful to the liver.
Why it was picked

A large randomized phase 2 trial tests 10 new four-drug regimens built on ganfeborole and BTZ-043 against standard therapy, a potential step toward shorter tuberculosis treatment.

Weekly semaglutide injections lowered BMI in children aged 6 to 11 with obesity

Semaglutide, a once-a-week injected drug, is already approved to treat obesity in teenagers and adults, but there has been little evidence on whether it works in younger children.

The New England Journal of Medicine · Nov 16, 2026

Original title: “Semaglutide for the Treatment of Obesity in Children 6 to <12 Years of Age”
Published Nov 16, 2026

Semaglutide, a once-a-week injected drug, is already approved to treat obesity in teenagers and adults, but there has been little evidence on whether it works in younger children. This study, called STEP Young, tested it in children from 6 to under 12 years old who have obesity, a condition the authors describe as a long-term disease involving the brain and metabolism, for which few effective and lasting medical treatments exist.

The researchers ran a phase 3 trial with 167 children. They were randomly assigned so that 112 got semaglutide and 55 got a placebo (a dummy injection), and neither the families nor the researchers knew who received which. After 68 weeks, average BMI (a weight-for-height measure) fell by 13.5% in the semaglutide group but rose by 2.7% in the placebo group. That works out to an estimated difference of about 16 percentage points, a gap the authors judged statistically significant.[2]

Why it matters

Young children with obesity have few medical treatment options, and this is one of the first rigorous trials of semaglutide in this age group. The results suggest the drug can substantially lower BMI in children younger than those it is currently approved for.

Caveats

The trial was fairly small (167 children), lasted 68 weeks, and was funded by the drug's manufacturer; the abstract provided does not report side effects or what happens after treatment stops.

Key terms
Semaglutide:
A medicine given as a weekly injection under the skin, already used to treat obesity in adolescents and adults.
BMI (body mass index):
A number calculated from a person's weight and height, used to judge whether their weight is in a healthy range.
Phase 3 trial:
A large-scale study testing whether a treatment works, typically done before it can be approved for a new use.
Double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled:
Participants are assigned to drug or dummy treatment by chance, and neither they nor the researchers know who got which, to reduce bias.
95% confidence interval:
A range of values within which the true effect is likely to lie; here the BMI difference was estimated to be between about 13 and 19 percentage points.
Why it was picked

The first phase 3 placebo-controlled trial of semaglutide in children aged 6 to under 12 showed a 13.5% mean BMI reduction, so it is included despite being five days old because of high public interest.

Google quantum chip wins cooperation games beyond any classical strategy

Can quantum effects let players coordinate better than any classical plan allows, even when they cannot talk to each other?

Science · Nov 19, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Mbqc-fig-single qubit pattern · Institute for Quantum Computing, Baidu Inc., via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “Quantum-classical separation in bounded-resource tasks arising from measurement contextuality”
Published Nov 19, 2026

Can quantum effects let players coordinate better than any classical plan allows, even when they cannot talk to each other? The Google Quantum AI team tested this on superconducting qubit processors (called Willow, with 72 and 105 qubits). The key idea is contextuality: in quantum mechanics, a measurement's result is not a fixed, pre-existing value but depends on which other measurements are made alongside it. They first used four qubits to play the 'magic square' game, where two separated players fill in a row and column of a 3×3 grid. The best classical strategy wins 8/9 of the time (about 89%), while the quantum team won about 98% of 100,000 rounds per game. They also measured a contextuality score that cannot exceed 4 classically; they got about 5.6, close to the ideal quantum value of 6.

They then scaled up. In a parity game for N players sharing an entangled 'GHZ' state, the measured win rate stayed above the classical limit for up to 71 players, though the margin shrank as more players were added. Finally, they solved a 'hidden linear function' problem on up to 105 qubits. In theory, a quantum circuit with a fixed, small number of layers solves this exactly, while classical circuits need more layers as the problem grows. Errors pushed the effective quantum depth from 4 to about 6 layers at 105 qubits, still below the authors' estimated classical requirement.[3]

Why it matters

Proving that quantum computers truly outperform classical ones is hard, and these tasks are among the few with mathematically proven separations under restricted resources. The work tests these ideas on real, noisy hardware at large scale and suggests contextuality-based tasks as a way to benchmark future quantum processors.

Caveats

This is a preprint not yet peer reviewed, so numbers in the final published paper may differ. The quantum edge shrinks as more qubits are added because of hardware errors, and the classical layer count used for the hidden linear function comparison is the authors' conjecture, since proven classical limits only apply at roughly a million qubits.

Key terms
Contextuality:
The quantum property that a measurement's outcome depends on which compatible measurements are done with it, so it cannot be assigned a fixed value in advance.
Magic square game:
A two-player game on a 3×3 grid of ±1 entries that no classical strategy can always win, but players sharing entangled qubits ideally can.
GHZ state:
An entangled state in which many qubits are all 0 and all 1 at the same time.
Bell–Kochen–Specker inequality:
A limit on certain combinations of measurement results that any theory with pre-set values must obey; quantum mechanics can break it.
Hidden linear function problem:
A math puzzle that a shallow quantum circuit provably solves with fewer layers than any classical circuit, for large enough sizes.
Superconducting qubit:
A quantum bit made from a tiny circuit cooled until it carries current without resistance.
Why it was picked

A superconducting-qubit experiment shows that measurement contextuality gives a provable quantum advantage in coordination tasks without communication.

A calcium catalyst shuttles fluoride ions to build fluorine-containing molecules

Many industrial catalysts rely on transition metals such as palladium, which work through well-understood steps where the metal grabs and releases molecular fragments.

Nature · Nov 19, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. · Benjah-bmm27, via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “Calcium catalysed nucleophilic fluorination”
Published Nov 19, 2026

Many industrial catalysts rely on transition metals such as palladium, which work through well-understood steps where the metal grabs and releases molecular fragments. Main-group metals like calcium are cheap and plentiful, but they cannot perform those familiar steps, so chemists need different ways to make them act as catalysts. This study asked whether calcium could catalyse nucleophilic fluorination, a reaction in which a fluoride ion (F⁻) replaces another group on a carbon atom.

The team designed a very bulky ligand, a large organic molecule carrying two negative charges, that wraps around calcium. This shape allows calcium to hold a single fluoride at the end of the complex, an unusual arrangement that is the key intermediate. The calcium complex picks up fluoride and then hands it to the target molecule, so it can repeat the cycle and act as a catalyst. In the slowest step of the reaction, the calcium–fluoride bond breaks late. That means calcium shapes how the fluoride behaves both electronically and physically. In early proof-of-concept tests, this control strongly favoured the desired substitution product over the competing elimination side reaction, a level of selectivity the authors describe as previously unseen.[4]

Why it matters

Fluorine-containing compounds are important in many products, and a catalyst built from abundant calcium could make their production more sustainable. The work also shows a general route to main-group catalysis: tune how the catalyst exists in solution and how strongly it binds its reagent.

Caveats

The authors describe the selectivity results as proof-of-concept, so the range of molecules the method works on and its practical scale-up are not established by the abstract.

Key terms
Nucleophilic fluorination:
A reaction in which a negatively charged fluoride ion attacks a carbon atom and replaces another group, attaching fluorine to the molecule.
Main-group elements:
Elements from the s- and p-blocks of the periodic table, such as calcium, as opposed to transition (d-block) metals.
Oxidative addition / reductive elimination:
Common steps in transition-metal catalysis in which the metal gains bonds and then gives them up again, changing its oxidation state; calcium cannot readily do this.
Ligand:
A molecule that binds to a metal atom. Here it is a large, crowded, doubly negative one that controls calcium's shape and reactivity.
Rate-determining transition state:
The highest-energy arrangement along the slowest step of a reaction, which sets the overall speed.
Substitution vs elimination:
Two competing outcomes. Fluoride can replace a group (substitution) or act as a base and remove atoms to form a double bond (elimination), giving an unwanted product.
Why it was picked

A new catalytic platform uses calcium, a cheap main-group metal, for nucleophilic fluorination, opening sustainable alternatives to transition-metal catalysts.

Astronomers weigh a distant black hole by watching gas swirl around it

Giant black holes at the centres of galaxies pull on the gas and stars close to them.

Science Advances · Nov 20, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Alma radio telescope · ESO/B. Tafreshi (twanight.org), via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY 4.0

Original title: “Resolving the black hole sphere of influence in a hyperluminous obscured quasar at redshift 4.6”
Published Nov 20, 2026

Giant black holes at the centres of galaxies pull on the gas and stars close to them. The region where the black hole's gravity dominates is called its sphere of influence, and tracking how gas moves inside it is one of the most reliable ways to measure a black hole's mass. Until now, this direct approach had not worked for black holes in the early universe, more than about 10 billion years ago (redshift above 2), because those regions are too small and faint to resolve.

The team made very sharp observations of a dust-hidden (obscured), extremely bright quasar at redshift 4.6, so we see it as it was when the universe was young. They mapped light given off by ionized carbon atoms and by carbon monoxide molecules, which let them trace gas motions down into the black hole's sphere of influence. The spread of gas speeds increased toward the centre in the way expected for material orbiting a central point mass, as planets do around the Sun. Fitting this pattern required a black hole of about 6 billion times the Sun's mass. The authors describe this as the first such gas-motion-based mass measurement beyond redshift 2.[5]

Why it matters

Most black hole masses in the early universe come from indirect estimates that rely on assumptions calibrated on nearby objects, so a direct gravitational measurement provides a more solid check. The authors suggest that heavily dust-shrouded quasars may protect the cold gas needed for this method, making them good targets for weighing more early black holes.

Caveats

This is a measurement of a single object, and it remains to be shown how many other early quasars can be resolved this way. The authors' idea that dust shielding helps the gas tracers survive near the black hole is a proposal, not something tested across many objects.

Key terms
Supermassive black hole (SMBH):
A black hole millions to billions of times the Sun's mass, found at the centre of most large galaxies.
Sphere of influence:
The region around a black hole where its gravity controls the motion of gas and stars more than the rest of the galaxy does.
Redshift (z):
How much an object's light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe; higher values mean more distant and seen earlier in cosmic history.
Obscured quasar:
A very bright, actively feeding black hole whose central light is hidden behind thick dust and gas.
Velocity dispersion:
The spread of speeds of gas at a given location; larger spread means stronger gravitational pull.
Keplerian rise:
The increase in gas speeds closer to a central mass, following the same orbital rules that govern planets around the Sun.
Why it was picked

This is the first dynamical measurement of a supermassive black hole mass beyond redshift 2, made by resolving its sphere of influence in a quasar at z=4.6.

How a hormone and a pill-like drug switch on the same heart receptor differently

RXFP1 is a receptor on cell surfaces that responds to relaxin, a natural hormone.

Science · Nov 19, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Human heart model · No machine-readable author provided. Ekko assumed (based on copyright claims)., via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “Divergent activation of the RXFP1 relaxin receptor by protein and small-molecule agonists”
Published Nov 19, 2026

RXFP1 is a receptor on cell surfaces that responds to relaxin, a natural hormone. Switching it on may help treat fibrosis (harmful scarring of tissue) and heart failure, and both protein-based and small-molecule drugs that activate it are already in mid-stage clinical trials for heart disease. The researchers wanted to know how these very different kinds of activators turn the receptor on. They used cryo-electron microscopy, which images frozen molecules to work out their 3D shape, to capture RXFP1 in three states: empty, bound to the natural hormone relaxin-2, and bound to the drug candidate AZD5462.

The structures show that the two activators grab different parts of the receptor. Relaxin-2 attaches to the ectodomain, the part sticking out of the cell, while AZD5462 sits in the transmembrane domain, the part embedded in the cell membrane. A second technique, hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, showed that relaxin-2 makes a connecting segment called the linker fold into a helix. AZD5462 instead holds the receptor in its active shape through a different set of structural shifts. So the hormone and the small molecule reach the same 'on' state by separate routes.[6]

Why it matters

Knowing exactly where and how each drug type acts on RXFP1 could guide the design of better heart and anti-fibrosis medicines. It also shows that one receptor can be activated through more than one mechanism, which matters for choosing between protein drugs and easier-to-take small molecules.

Caveats

These are structural and biochemical lab studies of the receptor; the abstract does not show how the different activation routes affect treatment outcomes in patients.

Key terms
Agonist:
A molecule that binds to a receptor and switches it on.
Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM):
A method that images rapidly frozen molecules with electrons to build detailed 3D models of their structure.
Ectodomain:
The part of a receptor that sits outside the cell.
Transmembrane domain:
The part of a receptor that crosses the cell's fatty outer membrane.
Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry:
A technique that tracks how readily parts of a protein swap hydrogen for heavy hydrogen, revealing which regions are flexible or change shape.
Fibrosis:
Excess build-up of scar-like tissue that stiffens organs.
Why it was picked

Cryo-EM structures show how the native hormone and a clinical small-molecule drug activate the relaxin receptor in different ways, relevant to heart failure and fibrosis therapies.

A clock-gene deletion helps heat-loving mosquitoes mate and carry more malaria

Anopheles stephensi is a mosquito that spreads malaria and has recently spread into Africa, where it copes well with hot city conditions. The researchers wanted to know what genetic changes let it handle the heat.

Cell · Nov 19, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. الأنوفيلة الاصْطِفانِيَّة · Jim Gathany, via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “A natural mutation of timeless enhances mosquito thermal adaptation and malaria parasite transmission”
Published Nov 19, 2026

Anopheles stephensi is a mosquito that spreads malaria and has recently spread into Africa, where it copes well with hot city conditions. The researchers wanted to know what genetic changes let it handle the heat. They kept mosquitoes at high temperatures in the lab for three years. Over that time, one version of the circadian clock gene timeless became common. The circadian clock is the internal system that keeps daily rhythms, and this version was missing a 5,000-letter stretch of DNA.

Males carrying one normal copy and one deleted copy of the gene (heterozygotes) kept steady activity of another clock gene, period, even in the heat. These males had higher activity of AKH1, a hormone gene that releases stored energy and boosts flight. They also had more activity of two enzyme genes, FAR3 and FAR5, which raised production of heptacosane, a chemical signal used in mating. Together these changes gave the males an edge in winning mates. Females with the same gene combination were more easily infected by Plasmodium, the malaria parasite, which suggests they could pass on more malaria.[7]

Why it matters

The work points to a possible genetic route by which an invasive malaria mosquito adapts to hot African cities while also becoming a better parasite carrier. Knowing this mechanism could help predict where the mosquito will thrive and guide malaria control efforts.

Caveats

The deletion became common in mosquitoes raised in the lab, so it is not yet clear how much it drives heat adaptation in wild African populations. The authors say the mechanism 'may' underlie adaptation, which marks it as a likely explanation rather than a proven one.

Key terms
Circadian clock:
An internal molecular timer that drives roughly 24-hour cycles in behaviour and body processes.
timeless (tim):
A core gene of the circadian clock; here, a version missing about 5,000 DNA letters was studied.
Heterozygous:
Having two different versions of a gene, one inherited from each parent.
Adipokinetic hormone (AKH1):
An insect hormone that releases stored fat and sugar as fuel, for example for flying.
Heptacosane pheromone:
A waxy chemical made by the mosquito that acts as a signal in mating.
Plasmodium:
The single-celled parasite that causes malaria and is spread by mosquitoes.
Why it was picked

A natural circadian-clock mutation in mosquitoes boosts mating at high temperatures and raises malaria susceptibility, linking climate adaptation to disease transmission.

Glacial lakes explain only a small share of glacier loss worldwide

When a glacier ends in a lake, the water can speed up melting and the breaking off of ice at the glacier's front.

Nature Climate Change · Nov 20, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. Glacial lake glacier · NASA, via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

Original title: “Climate and glacier geometry dominate global glacier–lake interactions”
Published Nov 20, 2026

When a glacier ends in a lake, the water can speed up melting and the breaking off of ice at the glacier's front. But it has not been clear how much these glacial lakes actually add to the ice that glaciers are losing around the world. To find out, the researchers mapped glacial lakes across the globe at two points in time, 2000 and 2020, and measured how their number, surface area and water volume changed.

Using these maps, they estimated that the lakes account for about 11% (give or take 7%) of the mass lost by glaciers that end in lakes. Most of the loss instead comes down to the climate (such as warming) and to the glacier's own shape and layout, which the authors identify as the main controls on how glaciers and lakes interact.[8]

Why it matters

Glacier loss raises sea levels and changes water supplies, so knowing what drives it helps improve forecasts. This study suggests that, although glacial lakes are growing, climate and glacier shape matter far more than the lakes themselves for how fast lake-ending glaciers shrink.

Caveats

The 11% estimate has a wide uncertainty range (±7%). Because the study compares only two snapshots, 2000 and 2020, it may miss shorter-term changes between them.

Key terms
Glacial lake:
A lake formed by glacier meltwater, often sitting against or in front of a glacier.
Lake-terminating glacier:
A glacier whose front edge ends in a lake rather than on dry land.
Glacier mass loss:
The amount of ice a glacier loses over time through melting and ice breaking off.
Climate forcing:
Outside climate influences, such as rising temperatures, that push a system like a glacier to change.
Glacier geometry:
The physical shape of a glacier, such as its size, slope and thickness.
Why it was picked

Global mapping of glacial lakes in 2000 and 2020 finds they explain about 11% of mass loss at lake-terminating glaciers, with climate and glacier geometry as the main drivers.

Simulations show a 'lens' trick could make light states with exactly 10,000+ photons

A Fock state is a state of light holding an exact, definite number of photons, unlike laser light, whose photon count fluctuates.

Physical Review Letters · Nov 19, 2026
Read more
Illustration, not from the paper. A niobium 1.3 GHz nine-cell superconducting radio frequency microwave cavity at Fermilab . · Z22, via Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

Original title: “Scalable Generation of Macroscopic Fock States Exceeding 10,000 Photons”
Published Nov 19, 2026

A Fock state is a state of light holding an exact, definite number of photons, unlike laser light, whose photon count fluctuates. Making Fock states with many photons is hard: past experiments and proposals have topped out at roughly a hundred photons, because the control needed grows more complex with photon number and large states lose photons to the environment faster. The authors asked whether a simple, scalable recipe could reach far larger photon numbers. They borrowed an idea from optics. Light hopping along a row of coupled waveguides behaves mathematically like a quantum state moving between neighbouring photon numbers. In that picture, a gentle push from a coherent drive (a steady external field) acts like light spreading out. The Kerr effect, where the light's phase shift depends on its own intensity, acts like a curved lens. Alternating the two can 'focus' a broad spread of photon numbers onto one value.

This is a theoretical and numerical study, not an experiment. Starting from ordinary laser-like light, a single 'Fock-space lens' sharpened the photon-number spread a lot. In one example, the top probability rose from 0.004 to 0.2. Stacking three lenses, with tuned settings that correct distortions much as a camera's lens group does, gave simulated fidelities (overlap with the ideal target state) of about 73% for 10,000 photons and about 62% for 100,000 photons. Fidelity fell only slightly as photon number grew. Unexpectedly, the time the procedure needs shrinks roughly as one over the square root of the photon number, which makes it more tolerant of photon loss. The authors outline possible versions in superconducting microwave circuits and in optical cavity setups.[9]

Why it matters

Light states with a large, exactly fixed photon number could let researchers probe where quantum behaviour gives way to classical behaviour. Such states could also improve the precision of quantum measurements. Because the recipe needs only a drive and a Kerr effect, and gets faster rather than slower for bigger states, it suggests a practical route that avoids the usual scaling problems.

Caveats

All results come from numerical simulations; no giant Fock state has yet been made in the lab, and the best simulated fidelities are still well below 100%. This summary is based on a preprint that had not been peer reviewed, so numbers in the final published paper may differ.

Key terms
Fock state:
A quantum state of light or vibration containing an exactly defined number of photons (or other quanta).
Coherent state:
The kind of state produced by a laser; its photon number is spread over a range of values rather than fixed.
Kerr nonlinearity:
An effect in which the phase a light field picks up depends on how many photons are present.
Fidelity:
A score from 0 to 1 measuring how closely the produced state matches the intended target state.
Aberration:
A distortion that stops a lens from focusing perfectly; here, an imperfection that blurs the photon-number focus.
SNAIL:
A type of superconducting circuit element that can provide an adjustable Kerr effect in microwave devices.

For more than two decades, researchers have widely held that in 'collisionless' plasmas (where particles rarely bump into each other) reconnection runs at a fixed fast rate of about 0.1 in standard units, no matter how large the system is. That belief came mostly from simulations of a textbook starting setup called a Harris current sheet, in which the sheet was always kept very thin while the simulation box was enlarged. Other setups, by contrast, showed the rate depending on size.

The authors argue that the earlier studies were not a fair test, because keeping the sheet thin while enlarging the box changes the overall shape of the magnetic field. They ran computer simulations with two methods, a particle-by-particle approach (particle-in-cell) and a simpler fluid model (Hall MHD), scaling the sheet thickness along with the box size so the overall geometry stayed the same. Under these conditions, the fixed fast rate disappeared: the reconnection rate fell as the system grew. In the particle simulations the rate dropped about 2.6-fold over a 30-fold size increase; in the fluid simulations it dropped about 3.4-fold over a 100-fold increase, with the decline becoming gentler at the largest sizes.[20]

Why it matters

Real solar flares and coronal current sheets are millions to billions of times larger than the smallest relevant plasma length, far beyond what simulations can reach, so how the rate changes with size controls how we extrapolate to the Sun and space. The authors note this fits loosely with observations: faster rates measured near Earth's relatively small magnetosphere and often slower rates inferred for solar flares.

Caveats

These are 2D simulations with a reduced ion-to-electron mass ratio, periodic boundaries and no guide field or 3D turbulence, and they reach sizes far smaller than real solar systems, so whether the decline continues or levels off is open; plasmoids also made the particle results noisy. The authors note that the biological mechanism by which low oxygen blocks mite reproduction is still unknown and needs further study. It is also not yet shown whether low-oxygen treatment would be practical or safe for bees as a real-world control method.

Fungi that attack crops cause large financial losses and threaten the world's food supply. One of the worst is Fusarium head blight, a fungal disease that infects the grain-bearing heads of wheat. The researchers wanted a new way to fight this disease.

Their approach uses carbon dots, which are extremely small carbon-based particles, made from plant material. These dots are photocatalytic, meaning they use light energy to drive chemical reactions. The short publisher description reports that this strategy works effectively against the fungus, but it does not explain how the dots harm the fungus, how they were tested, or how well they performed.[39]

Why it matters

Wheat is a staple food for much of the world, so diseases that damage it put food security at risk. A treatment made from plant material and powered by light could offer another option for managing fungal crop diseases.

Caveats

The description is very brief. It does not say whether the method was tested in the lab, in greenhouses or in real fields, nor does it address cost, safety or effects on the environment.
